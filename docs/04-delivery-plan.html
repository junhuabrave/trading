Delivery plan to go-live

Document 4. Every remaining task as an assignable work item with its dependencies, its size, and the test that says it is finished. Written so several engineers can work in parallel against the contracts that already exist, and so the things that actually gate the launch date stay visible.

How to use this

Documents 1 and 2 are the design. Document 3 is the gap analysis and the architecture of each missing component; it is what an engineer reads before starting an item. This document is the delivery plan: the same work cut into pieces one person can own, with the order they have to happen in and the evidence that closes them.

Every item has an id (MD-3, RT-2), the items it depends on, a size in engineer-weeks for someone who has read document 3 and is comfortable in the codebase, and a done when that is a command or a check, not an opinion. An item is finished when its done when is green in CI on the main branch, not when the code is merged.

Sizes assume one engineer per item, with review. They do not include the ramp-up: budget a week for the first item anyone takes, spent reading document 2 and 3, running make test, and walking one order through the simulator with tools/loginspect.py chain.

The estimate to take seriously. The engineering below is about 256 engineer-weeks that block go-live, plus 55 more that follow it. Sixteen engineers do not finish that in sixteen weeks: the dependency chain through market data and the router is much longer than the average, so plan on twelve to fourteen months. The regulatory, clearing and venue-certification track in the non-software section runs twelve to eighteen months from a standing start and is almost always what sets the date. Start that track first, whatever the engineering plan says.

The first week on a track

The same for everyone, and worth not skipping. Clone, run make test, and watch the seven simulator scenarios pass. Read document 2 for the wire format and document 3 for your component. Then take one order from a simulated day and follow it end to end:

make test                                    # everything green before you change anything
python3 tools/loginspect.py stats build/sim/random-day/core.jnl
python3 tools/loginspect.py chain build/sim/random-day/core.jnl <seq of a fill>
make bench                                   # your component will add a row here

That chain command prints the order's life as a waterfall: client order, risk decision, route decision, child order, venue ack, fill, client report, each with the time it took. Once that reads as obvious, so does the codebase.

Each track then has a first item chosen to be self-contained and to teach the contracts, rather than to be on the critical path.

Rules for parallel work

These are what let six people work at once without breaking each other. They are not style preferences; each one has bitten this codebase or is the reason something in it is testable.

The schema is a contract, and it is append-only

Adding a field or message is normal and cheap. Changing or reordering an existing one is not allowed, and make check will refuse it. New fields come out of a message's trailing padding so the block length never changes, carry sinceVersion, and default to zero meaning "unset". Bump the version, record the reason in schema/CHANGELOG.md, and freeze the previous copy under schema/versions/. Never edit gen/.

Two people adding messages in the same week is fine; they pick template ids from different family blocks and the checker catches a collision.

Engines talk only through sequenced messages

No hot-path component calls another, shares memory with it, reads a clock, opens a socket, or touches a database. Everything it knows arrives as a frame; everything it decides leaves as a frame. That is what makes replay exact, and it is also what lets you develop your component against the simulator before the component that feeds it exists.

If your component seems to need something that is not on the stream, the answer is a new message, not a side channel.

Every engine ships with a replay-determinism test

Same journal in, same state hash out, verified by a cold replay in the harness, and the engine answers each Checkpoint with a CheckpointAck carrying its hash. An engine without this is not finished, because nothing downstream can trust it and no failover can use it.

Every hot-path component ships with a benchmark row and a gate

Add your component's benchmark to tools/bench.py, record a baseline, and keep the allocation count at zero after warm-up. The gate is what stops the stack getting slower one reasonable-looking commit at a time. On a shared CI runner it is informational; on the pinned host it fails the build.

The simulator is where your component is tested, and you extend it

Do not write a bespoke harness. Add your scenario or drill to sim/, give it a stored baseline, and put it in make test. When your component needs something the simulator cannot yet do (a second venue, a dropped multicast line), extending the simulator is part of your item, not a separate project.

No allocation, no syscalls, no wall clock on the order path

Tables are open-addressing and reserved at construction (core/util/flatmap.hpp), time comes from the injected clock, and logging goes through a ring drained elsewhere. The benchmark's allocation counter and the strace check in CI enforce the first two.

Cold-path services are Go, and they consume the log

The generated Go codec is in gen/go. A cold service subscribes to the sequenced log and keeps its own state; it never queries another service for state that is on the log, and it is allowed to be slow but never to be wrong.

Reason codes are the vocabulary

Any new reject or decision reason goes in schema/reasons.csv and must be produced by at least one test; tools/reason_coverage.py in make test enforces it. A code nobody can trigger is either dead or untested.

Critical path

The chain that decides the date runs through market data. Nothing in the router can be built against a synthetic book once the real one exists, and best-execution reporting cannot be finished until the router is. Everything else parallelises around it.

Three things worth reading off the diagram. Market data to router to best-execution reporting is eleven months of chain and cannot be compressed by adding people to the middle of it. Venue certification starts before the gateways are finished, because the queue is the constraint rather than the code. And the parallel run at the end is twenty business days of clean reconciliation against real market data with no client money at risk; it is not optional and it is not shortenable.

What will go wrong

Named so the team recognises them early rather than discovering them.

  • Venue certification queues. Measured in months, scheduled by the venue, and not compressible with money or effort. Book slots before the gateway is finished; a failed slot costs weeks of waiting, not days of fixing.
  • Market-data licensing arithmetic. Direct-feed costs scale with client count in ways that can invert the business model. Get the real per-client numbers in writing during the first quarter, not during the pricing discussion with the first client.
  • The clearing firm's risk appetite. A new broker with a market maker and a stock-loan book is a harder sell than a plain agency broker. Have a second candidate in diligence, because the first one saying no late is a six-month event.
  • Reconciliation breaks late in the parallel run. The twenty-day clock restarts on an unexplained break. Run reconciliation from the day the ledger exists, not from the day the parallel run starts, so the breaks are found in month six rather than month thirteen.
  • Determinism erosion. The first component someone writes with a map iteration, a wall clock, or an unordered container in a state hash breaks replay, and it will not fail loudly. The checkpoint-hash comparison across builds is what catches it; keep it in CI and treat a divergence as a stop-work.
  • The tail, not the median. Clients measure the worst fifty in a hundred thousand. Every allocation, every syscall and every lock that creeps onto the order path shows up there and nowhere else. The benchmark gates exist for this; do not let them go informational on the pinned host.
  • Scope creep into market making. It is the most interesting work in the building and it is deliberately after launch. Adding it early costs the launch date and doubles the compliance surface at exactly the moment the firm has no track record to defend it with.
  • Hiring is the first dependency, and the plan is silent about it. Every estimate here starts when the engineer exists. Sixteen people, several of them hot-path C++ engineers with exchange-protocol experience, is a six-month recruiting programme in a thin market, and a track that starts three months late finishes three months late. Sequence the hiring to the critical path: market data and platform first, compliance and post-trade next, market making last.
  • The plan's own blind spots. The first draft of this document omitted the accounts and onboarding component entirely, left clock synchronisation, timers, alert routing and capacity testing without an owner, and stated a headline number that contradicted its own staffing table. All are corrected above. The lesson is not that those items were hard; it is that a plan assembled from component designs inherits their silences. Re-read this document against document 1's component list each quarter and ask what has no id.
  • Key-person concentration. Every track above has one owner. Write down what each of them knows that is not in the repository, and pair on the sequencer, the risk engine and the clearing adapter specifically.
